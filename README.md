## Crypto in context: from origins to today

Crypto is not one thing. It combines monetary networks, programmable blockchains, tokens, decentralized applications, market infrastructure, digital ownership, and public-sector experiments. The same asset can appear in several places: a token may be issued on a smart-contract chain, traded on an exchange, tracked by a block explorer, used in a DeFi protocol, and represented by an NFT or a wallet.

### A short history

- **2008–2009 — the starting point:** Bitcoin introduced a peer-to-peer electronic-cash design using a public ledger and proof-of-work. The [Bitcoin whitepaper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf) and [Bitcoin Core source](https://github.com/bitcoin/bitcoin) are the primary references.
- **2013–2015 — programmable blockchains:** Ethereum extended the idea from a payment network to a general-purpose platform for smart contracts and decentralized applications. Read the [Ethereum whitepaper](https://ethereum.org/en/whitepaper/) and [developer docs](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/).
- **2016–2019 — interoperability, privacy, and token standards:** New networks explored proof-of-stake, cross-chain communication, privacy technologies, and standards for issuing fungible tokens and NFTs.
- **2020–2021 — DeFi and NFTs:** Automated market makers, lending markets, liquid staking, yield strategies, and NFT marketplaces became major application categories. Protocol docs and smart-contract repositories matter more than promotional token pages.
- **2022 onward — scaling, data, security, and real-world assets:** Layer-2 networks, modular infrastructure, bridges, oracle networks, tokenized funds, stablecoin regulation, and security research became central parts of the ecosystem. The relevant current sources are organized in the directory rather than frozen into a single ranking.
- **Today:** Use the directory as a map, not a leaderboard. For current token names, prices, supply events, holders, liquidity, and chain-specific contract pages, cross-check multiple services and then follow the project’s official documentation. Market-data websites are discovery tools, not primary proof of legitimacy.

### How the pieces fit together

1. **Networks** provide consensus, settlement, and execution: Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, Cosmos, and many others.
2. **Tokens** represent native network assets, stable value, governance rights, utility, or claims on an application. A token is not automatically an equity share or a safe investment.
3. **Smart contracts and protocols** implement exchanges, lending, derivatives, staking, identity, games, and other applications.
4. **Infrastructure** includes wallets, nodes, SDKs, RPC providers, oracles, indexing, explorers, bridges, and security tooling.
5. **Discovery and measurement** includes Etherscan token pages, market-data directories, DeFiLlama, DEX Screener, analytics dashboards, and research providers. These should be treated as sources to investigate, not as endorsements.
6. **Public-sector digital money** includes CBDC research and pilots. These are usually centrally issued and governed, so they should not be conflated with permissionless cryptocurrencies.

### A practical research path

- Start with the project’s **official site and documentation**.
- Confirm the **official source-code organization** and inspect recent activity, releases, issues, and license.
- Read the **whitepaper or technical specification**, but also check whether the implementation matches the claims.
- Use an **explorer** such as [Etherscan Tokens](https://etherscan.io/tokens) to inspect token metadata, holders, transfers, verified contracts, and chain context. Verification badges are useful signals, not guarantees.
- Cross-check market and protocol data with [CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com), [CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com), [DefiLlama](https://defillama.com), or [DEX Screener](https://dexscreener.com). Look for differences in definitions, chain coverage, and update timing.
- Treat unverified token pages, anonymous teams, unrealistic yield claims, copycat domains, and requests for seed phrases or private keys as red flags.

## Inclusion and quality notes

- Entries are selected from the two upstream repositories plus widely referenced official ecosystems, infrastructure, analytics services, and public-sector resources needed to make the map useful today.
- Preference is given to primary sources: official project sites, official documentation, official source-code organizations, standards, central banks, and established research institutions.
- This is a curated directory, not an endorsement. It does not guarantee safety, legality, liquidity, decentralization, profitability, or continued maintenance.
- “Government” entries are included as **public-sector digital-currency resources**. A CBDC is not the same thing as a permissionless cryptocurrency.
- Suspicious promotional sites, anonymous token launches, unverified contract addresses, and unsupported investment claims are intentionally excluded.
